1-DOF and 2-DOF Spring-System Analysis

The useful result is not just a set of frequencies. An ideal vibration model predicts the structure of the response, while real hardware shifts the measured frequencies through damping, distributed spring mass, sensor mass, and coupled motion.

1-DOF insight

The single-spring test gives natural frequency and damping, but the FFT and peak-count methods reveal different views of the same physical response.

2-DOF insight

The two-spring test requires an eigenvalue model because the motion separates into multiple modal frequencies and mode shapes.

Experimental insight

The accelerometer data validates the theory and also exposes where the printed spring assembly is not a perfect lumped-parameter system.

Good morning, my name is Kayle Megginson, and today I am going to walk through my vibration analysis of a printed spring system in both a one-degree-of-freedom and two-degree-of-freedom setup. The main goal was not just to calculate a frequency and call it done. I wanted to see how well the ideal vibration models describe the real hardware once the spring, masses, sensors, wiring, and damping are all part of the test. So throughout the presentation, I will use the theory as the reference point, then compare it with what the accelerometer data actually showed. The big takeaway is that the model does a good job predicting the overall behavior, but the experimental results are more complicated in useful ways. Those differences help explain what is really happening in the physical system.

I organized the analysis so it follows the same path as the experiment. First I define the hardware and the measured quantities, then I build the mathematical model, and finally I compare the predictions with the data. For the single-spring case, I look at the natural frequency, the damped response, the peak-count estimate, and the FFT result. For the two-spring case, the problem changes from one equation into a matrix model, so the important results are the two modal frequencies and the mode shapes. That structure makes the comparison easier, because every number in the results can be traced back either to a measured value or to a specific modeling assumption.

The measurement setup was intentionally simple. I used an Arduino Nano to collect acceleration data from GY-521 sensor boards, then processed that signal later for the time response and frequency response. The sensors communicate over I2C, which is helpful because the two-degree-of-freedom setup can use two accelerometers on the same bus. One sensor stays at the default address, and the second one is shifted by changing the AD0 connection. The key point here is that the hardware is part of the experiment, not just a way to record it. The sensor boards, wires, tape, and mounting method all add small physical effects, so I kept those in mind when interpreting differences between the model and the measurements.

System Setup

One spring for 1-DOF, two springs for coupled motion

The physical setups separate the single-coordinate oscillator from the coupled two-coordinate system.

These photos show the two test configurations. The first setup is the simpler case: one spring, one moving mass, and one main coordinate describing the motion. The second setup adds another spring and another mass, which changes the behavior completely. Once the two masses can move relative to each other, the system can vibrate in different patterns instead of behaving like one isolated oscillator. This is also where the physical details start to matter. The real setup includes mounted electronics and flexible connections, so the experiment is close to the model, but it is not a perfect textbook system.

Before comparing theory and experiment, I needed a consistent set of inputs. The data was sampled at about 84.7 hertz, which sets the resolution for the recorded signal. From the single-degree-of-freedom response, I used two different frequency estimates: one from counting peaks in time and one from the FFT. I also calculated damping from logarithmic decrement. That gives a damping ratio of about 0.048, so the system is lightly damped. This will be important later because the damping correction changes the theoretical frequency only slightly.

1-DOF Theory

The free-vibration model predicts frequency and damping

The damping correction is included, but it is small because the measured damping ratio is much less than one.

\[ \begin{aligned} m\ddot{x}+c\dot{x}+kx&=0\\ \omega_n&=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},\qquad f_n=\frac{\omega_n}{2\pi} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{106.6}{0.0304}}=59.216\ \mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad f_n=\frac{59.216}{2\pi}=9.425\ \mathrm{Hz} \]
\[ \delta=\frac{1}{N}\ln\left(\frac{x_i}{x_{i+N}}\right)=0.30120,\qquad \zeta=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}}=0.04788 \]
\[ f_d=f_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}=9.414\ \mathrm{Hz} \]

1-DOF frequency comparison

  • Theoretical \(\omega_n\)59.216 rad/s
  • Theoretical \(f_n\)9.425 Hz
  • Damped \(f_d\)9.414 Hz
  • Peak-count result11.012 Hz
  • FFT result8.466 Hz
  • Damping ratio0.04788
Because \(f_d\) is almost equal to \(f_n\), damping alone does not explain the difference between peak counting and FFT.
For the one-degree-of-freedom model, I treated the setup as a mass, spring, and damper in free vibration. Using the measured mass and stiffness, the undamped natural frequency comes out to 9.425 hertz. Then I included damping using logarithmic decrement. Since the damping ratio is only about 0.048, the damped frequency drops only slightly, from 9.425 hertz to 9.414 hertz. That small change is important. If the measured frequencies differ from theory by a larger amount, damping alone cannot be the full explanation. It means other real-hardware effects are also influencing the response.

1-DOF Results

Peak counting and FFT show different sides of the response

The time-domain method approximates the response as one clean oscillator; the FFT shows the dominant frequency content of the real assembly.

The disagreement is useful: it shows that the physical printed spring setup is richer than a perfect 1-DOF model.
Here is where the comparison becomes interesting. The peak-count method gives a higher frequency than the theoretical model, while the FFT gives a lower dominant frequency. I do not see that disagreement as a failure of the experiment. I see it as evidence that the two methods are looking at the response differently. Peak counting follows the visible oscillations in the time signal, while the FFT identifies the strongest frequency content over the full record. Since the real spring has distributed mass and the sensor adds inertia at the end, the response is not perfectly described by a single ideal coordinate. That is why the difference between the methods is useful instead of just being random error.

The raw time and FFT plots support the measured results

The time-domain response and FFT spectrum answer different questions about the same free-vibration test.

These two plots are the evidence behind the 1-DOF measurements. On the left, the time response shows the decaying motion after release. That is the signal I used for peak counting and damping. 2-DOF Theory

The coupled system is solved as an eigenvalue problem

Two masses and two springs produce two theoretical natural frequencies and two mode shapes.

\[ M= \begin{bmatrix} 0.0304 & 0\\ 0 & 0.0101 \end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg}, \qquad K= \begin{bmatrix} 213.2 & -106.6\\ -106.6 & 106.6 \end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{N/m} \]
\[ \det(K-\omega^2M)= \begin{vmatrix} 213.2-0.0304\omega^2 & -106.6\\ -106.6 & 106.6-0.0101\omega^2 \end{vmatrix}=0 \]
\[ (213.2-0.0304\omega^2)(106.6-0.0101\omega^2)-(106.6)^2=0 \]
\[ 0.00030704\omega^4-5.39396\omega^2+11363.56=0 \]
\[ \omega_1=49.475\ \mathrm{rad/s},\quad \omega_2=122.963\ \mathrm{rad/s},\quad f_i=\frac{\omega_i}{2\pi} \]

Eigenvalue result

  • \(\omega_1\)49.475 rad/s
  • \(\omega_2\)122.963 rad/s
  • Mode 17.874 Hz
  • Mode 219.570 Hz
  • \(\phi_1\)[0.768, 1.000]
  • \(\phi_2\)[0.433, -1.000]
The measured FFT value of 8.466 Hz is closest to the predicted first mode.
For the two-degree-of-freedom setup, the math has to account for both masses moving at the same time. Instead of one scalar equation, I used mass and stiffness matrices. The stiffness matrix includes the coupling between the two coordinates. That coupling is the reason the system has two natural frequencies instead of one. Solving the eigenvalue problem gives a lower mode at 7.874 hertz and a higher mode at 19.570 hertz. The lower mode represents motion where the masses move mostly together, and the higher mode represents motion where they move against each other. So before looking at the data, the model already predicts that the measured response should not be just one simple frequency. It should contain modal behavior.

2-DOF Results

The lower predicted mode is closest to the FFT statistic

The analytical model predicts both a lower in-phase mode and a higher out-of-phase mode.

When I compare the two-degree-of-freedom model with the measured FFT value, the measured frequency is closest to the first predicted mode. That makes sense for a free-vibration test because the way the system is released can excite one mode more strongly than the other. The second mode is still part of the theoretical system, but it may not appear as strongly in the measured spectrum depending on the initial motion and sensor placement. The mode-shape plot helps explain the difference between the two modes. In the first mode, the masses move mostly together. In the second mode, their motion has opposite signs, which means the masses move against each other.

2-DOF Signal Evidence

The accelerometer signals show coupled motion

The time response shows both accelerometer channels, while the FFT spectrum separates the coupled signal into frequency content.

The time response here shows why the 2-DOF system is more complex than the single-spring case. The motion of one mass affects the other, so the response is coupled instead of independent. The FFT is useful because it separates that combined motion into frequency content. Even if the time signal looks messy, the frequency plot can still show which modes are being excited. Using two accelerometer channels also gives more information than a single sensor would. It helps connect the measured frequencies back to the relative motion between the two masses, which is what a mode shape represents.

Discussion and Conclusion

The ideal model predicts the trend; the experiment reveals the real system

The measured differences are physically meaningful rather than just error. They point to damping, distributed mass, sensor mass, assembly compliance, and measurement-method sensitivity.

1-DOF conclusion

The theoretical frequency is 9.425 Hz, but peak counting and FFT give different measured values. That means the real oscillator is not perfectly ideal.

2-DOF conclusion

The mass and stiffness matrices predict two modes. The measured FFT value aligns most closely with the lower theoretical mode.

Hardware conclusion

Arduino accelerometer data makes the theory testable, while the physical printed spring and mounted electronics explain the deviations.

\[ \text{Theory gives the model; experiment tells us how the real system behaves.} \]
To close, the main result is that the theory predicted the overall behavior, but the experiment showed the limits of the ideal assumptions. In the 1-DOF case, the theoretical frequency landed between the two measured estimates. That tells me the model is useful, but the physical setup has effects that the simple lumped-parameter equation does not fully capture. In the 2-DOF case, the eigenvalue model predicted two modes, and the measured FFT value lined up most closely with the lower one. That supports the idea that the real response contains modal behavior, even though the hardware and measurement method affect what shows up most clearly. Overall, the experiment reinforced the value of the vibration model while also showing why real measurements matter. The equations explain the trend, and the hardware tells us what the system actually does.
