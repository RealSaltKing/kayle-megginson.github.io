1-DOF and 2-DOF Spring-System Analysis

The useful result is not just a set of frequencies. An ideal vibration model predicts the structure of the response, while real hardware shifts the measured frequencies through damping, distributed spring mass, sensor mass, and coupled motion.

1-DOF insight

The single-spring test gives natural frequency and damping, but the FFT and peak-count methods reveal different views of the same physical response.

2-DOF insight

The two-spring test requires an eigenvalue model because the motion separates into multiple modal frequencies and mode shapes.

Experimental insight

The accelerometer data validates the theory and also exposes where the printed spring assembly is not a perfect lumped-parameter system.

This presentation analyzes one-degree-of-freedom and two-degree-of-freedom spring systems. The main idea is that theory predicts the structure of the response, but the experiment shows how the real hardware changes the exact numbers. For the 1-DOF system, I use the free-vibration model to estimate natural frequency and damping. For the 2-DOF system, I use a matrix eigenvalue model to predict multiple modes. The central insight is that real systems include damping, sensor mass, wiring, tape, support compliance, and distributed spring mass, so the measured response is more complicated than the ideal model.

1-DOF Theory

The free-vibration model predicts frequency and damping

The damping correction is included, but it is small because the measured damping ratio is much less than one.

\[ \begin{aligned} m\ddot{x}+c\dot{x}+kx&=0\\ \omega_n&=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},\qquad f_n=\frac{\omega_n}{2\pi} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{106.6}{0.0304}}=59.216\ \mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad f_n=\frac{59.216}{2\pi}=9.425\ \mathrm{Hz} \]
\[ \delta=\frac{1}{N}\ln\left(\frac{x_i}{x_{i+N}}\right)=0.30120,\qquad \zeta=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}}=0.04788 \]
\[ f_d=f_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}=9.414\ \mathrm{Hz} \]

1-DOF frequency comparison

  • Theoretical \(\omega_n\)59.216 rad/s
  • Theoretical \(f_n\)9.425 Hz
  • Damped \(f_d\)9.414 Hz
  • Peak-count result11.012 Hz
  • FFT result8.466 Hz
  • Damping ratio0.04788
Because \(f_d\) is almost equal to \(f_n\), damping alone does not explain the difference between peak counting and FFT.
For the 1-DOF system, the governing equation is m x double dot plus c x dot plus k x equals zero. The theoretical natural frequency is omega n equals the square root of k over m. Substituting the measured values gives omega n equal to 59.216 radians per second. Converting that angular frequency to cycles per second by dividing by 2 pi gives 9.425 hertz. I also used logarithmic decrement to compute damping. With delta equal to 0.30120, the damping ratio is 0.04788. The damped frequency is 9.414 hertz, so the damping correction is small. That means damping is not enough by itself to explain why peak counting and FFT give different measured frequencies.

2-DOF Theory

The coupled system is solved as an eigenvalue problem

Two masses and two springs produce two theoretical natural frequencies and two mode shapes.

\[ M= \begin{bmatrix} 0.0304 & 0\\ 0 & 0.0101 \end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg}, \qquad K= \begin{bmatrix} 213.2 & -106.6\\ -106.6 & 106.6 \end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{N/m} \]
\[ \det(K-\omega^2M)= \begin{vmatrix} 213.2-0.0304\omega^2 & -106.6\\ -106.6 & 106.6-0.0101\omega^2 \end{vmatrix}=0 \]
\[ (213.2-0.0304\omega^2)(106.6-0.0101\omega^2)-(106.6)^2=0 \]
\[ 0.00030704\omega^4-5.39396\omega^2+11363.56=0 \]
\[ \omega_1=49.475\ \mathrm{rad/s},\quad \omega_2=122.963\ \mathrm{rad/s},\quad f_i=\frac{\omega_i}{2\pi} \]

Eigenvalue result

  • \(\omega_1\)49.475 rad/s
  • \(\omega_2\)122.963 rad/s
  • Mode 17.874 Hz
  • Mode 219.570 Hz
  • \(\phi_1\)[0.768, 1.000]
  • \(\phi_2\)[0.433, -1.000]
The measured FFT value of 8.466 Hz is closest to the predicted first mode.
For the 2-DOF system, the equation becomes a matrix equation: M times x double dot plus K times x equals zero. The mass matrix contains the two measured masses, 0.0304 kilograms and 0.0101 kilograms. Since both springs use the baseline stiffness of 106.6 newtons per meter, the stiffness matrix has 213.2 in the first diagonal term, negative 106.6 coupling terms, and 106.6 in the second diagonal term. The natural frequencies come from the determinant of K minus omega squared M equals zero. Substituting the actual mass and stiffness values gives the determinant matrix with 213.2 minus 0.0304 omega squared in the first diagonal entry, 106.6 minus 0.0101 omega squared in the second diagonal entry, and negative 106.6 in the off-diagonal coupling terms. Expanding that determinant gives 0.00030704 omega to the fourth, minus 5.39396 omega squared, plus 11363.56 equals zero. Solving that polynomial gives omega one equal to 49.475 radians per second and omega two equal to 122.963 radians per second. Dividing by 2 pi gives a first mode at 7.874 hertz and a second mode at 19.570 hertz.

Discussion and Conclusion

The ideal model predicts the trend; the experiment reveals the real system

The measured differences are physically meaningful rather than just error. They point to damping, distributed mass, sensor mass, assembly compliance, and measurement-method sensitivity.

1-DOF conclusion

The theoretical frequency is 9.425 Hz, but peak counting and FFT give different measured values. That means the real oscillator is not perfectly ideal.

2-DOF conclusion

The mass and stiffness matrices predict two modes. The measured FFT value aligns most closely with the lower theoretical mode.

Hardware conclusion

Arduino accelerometer data makes the theory testable, while the physical printed spring and mounted electronics explain the deviations.

\[ \text{Theory gives the model; experiment tells us how the real system behaves.} \]
My final conclusion is that the experiment supports the vibration theory, but also shows the limits of the ideal model. For the 1-DOF system, the theoretical natural frequency is 9.425 hertz. The peak-count value and FFT value do not match that exactly, which suggests that the real assembly is not a perfect single-degree-of-freedom oscillator. For the 2-DOF system, the eigenvalue model predicts two modes. The measured FFT statistic is closest to the lower mode, which is consistent with the idea that the transient response contains modal behavior. The main sources of discrepancy are sensor mass, wiring, tape, support compliance, distributed spring mass, and the fact that peak counting and FFT measure the response in different ways. Overall, theory gives the model, and experiment tells us how the real system behaves.
