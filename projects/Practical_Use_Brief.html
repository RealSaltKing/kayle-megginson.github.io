Kayle Megginson | WR 545 Project 2 | Based on Practical Use Brief Vehicle Longevity Project.

For this project, I used computing to analyze a mechanical engineering question: how long do different types of vehicles stay useful? I looked at EPA vehicle data and compared lifespan patterns across fuel types and design characteristics. The goal was not just to make a graph, but to understand how data can help engineers make better decisions about durability, maintenance, cost, and environmental impact.

Why This Matters

Longevity changes the real cost of a vehicle.

  • Vehicles that last longer can reduce replacement demand and ownership cost.
  • Longer lifespan can also extend emissions from older technology.
  • Engineers need evidence to balance durability, efficiency, and environmental impact.
Vehicle longevity matters because it changes the real cost and impact of a vehicle. If a vehicle lasts longer, owners and fleets may spend less on replacement, and manufacturers may reduce the need to build as many new vehicles. But there is also a tradeoff. Keeping older, less efficient vehicles on the road can extend emissions from older technology. That makes lifespan an engineering issue, not just a consumer preference.

The basic idea of my analysis was to estimate how long vehicles remain on the road. I compared groups based on fuel type, engine size, and efficiency. My workflow started with the EPA Fuel Economy dataset, which included vehicle models from 1985 through 2024. I used R to clean the data, group vehicles by fuel type, estimate vehicle age, and create charts that showed how different groups changed over time. After that, I translated the patterns into engineering language so the results could support decisions about durability, replacement planning, and lifecycle cost.

Main Finding

Fuel type was a strong predictor of vehicle longevity.

  • Diesel vehicles showed the strongest long-term pattern in the lifespan curve.
  • Midgrade gasoline vehicles had the shortest median lifespan in the dataset.
  • Regular gasoline had the largest evidence base, with 30,374 records.

The plot shows that the vehicle groups separate over time instead of following one shared lifespan pattern.

This is the main finding from the project. The curve shows that the fuel groups do not all follow the same lifespan pattern. Diesel vehicles showed a strong long-term pattern, while midgrade gasoline vehicles had a much shorter median lifespan in the dataset. Regular gasoline had the largest evidence base, with more than thirty thousand records. I would not say fuel type is the only cause of these differences, because maintenance, usage, and vehicle design also matter. But the data does show that fuel type is meaningfully connected to how long vehicles remain useful.
